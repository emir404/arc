## 1. Proposed argument (a hypothesis to test in the essay, not a foregone conclusion)

The evidence supports a two-level, time-qualified answer:

1. Concentration is real, but its form changed. Between 2021 and 2023 a handful of venture rounds set the annual totals: Getir and Dream Games were about 94% of 2021 volume (Scalex citing Startups.watch) [1]; Getir's $768M round was 48% of 2022 volume and its $500M down round was 69% of 2023 volume (computed from Startups.watch totals) [2+]. From 2024 the concentration shows up differently: nine rounds of $20M or more took 81% of the value in the first nine months of 2024 [1]; in 2025 fintech and gaming took 68% of value and no later-stage round was recorded at all [2+]; and in 2024 to 2026 the largest "startup investment" figures reported by KPMG and 212 are acquisitions (Hepsiburada, Trendyol Go, Loom Games, Getir units), which are exits rather than growth capital. So "increasing" is only defensible for the share of value captured by foreign-led large transactions (foreign investors: 12% of deals but about 83% of value in 2025; 90 to 97% of value in the first half of 2026) [1, 2+]. By company, concentration peaked in 2021 to 2023 and eased in 2025 ("absence of mega-deals").

2. Firm level: concentrated capital demonstrably raises scalability and short-term survival for the recipients. Insider's $500M Series E (2024) was explicitly for AI investment and US expansion; Dream Games scaled Royal Match globally through three rounds in 2021 and 2022 and reached an approximately $5B private-equity transaction in 2025; Midas went from a $45M Series A (2024) to an $80M Series B (2025) at about $1B; Grand Games (founded 2024) went from a $3M pre-seed (July 2024) to a $30M Series A (January 2025) and a $70M Series B (May 2026), a total of $103M in 22 months. Mechanisms: runway, hiring and user-acquisition spend, international expansion, signalling to later foreign investors, professionalisation. The academic literature supports the runway and monitoring channels (Puri and Zarutskie, 2012; Bernstein, Giroud and Townsend, 2016; Hellmann and Puri, 2002). Getir is the counter-case: abundant capital enabled over-expansion, a 4x valuation cut and retreat, consistent with the hot-market evidence of Nanda and Rhodes-Kropf (2013).

3. Survival: the effect is observable only in the short term and only as proxies. No dataset of VC-backed startup survival exists for Türkiye; TÜİK business demography gives general enterprise survival (78.3% one-year survival for 2023 births; 58.6% two-year survival for 2022 births) but nothing for technology startups. Follow-on funding is the best proxy, and here the funnel is extreme: 284 seed rounds versus 22 early-stage rounds and zero later-stage rounds in 2025 [1]; a reported seed-to-early-stage conversion of about 7% versus about 50% in the UK, France and Germany [S, definition unverified]. Reverse causality is central: Sørensen (2007) finds sorting about twice as important as influence, and Turkish foreign investors are described as "more interested in mature companies", so recipients are selected for scalability before they receive capital.

4. Ecosystem level: the seed-heavy, Series-A-light funnel looks like a financing bottleneck, but attributing it to concentration is weak. The bottleneck predates 2020 (a 2015 cohort converted at about 9.7%), coincides with the 2023 to 2024 macro shock (policy rate to 50%, inflation peak 85.5%) and with the global correction, is partly statistical (companies that raise Series A move their legal seat abroad, so later rounds are booked elsewhere), and seed counts are inflated by TÜBİTAK BiGG grants (about 228 of 247 seed deals in the first nine months of 2024). Concentration is better read as a symptom of a two-tier market (domestic small tickets and grants at seed; foreign capital for a few globally scalable companies) than as its cause.

5. New funds: Revo Capital Fund III ($86M first close, April 2025, $100M+ target, IFC, DEG and EBRD as LPs), e2vc Fund III (€100M), Laton Ventures ($50M) and record numbers of GSYFs (478 active by mid-2025) add early-stage supply, but with global-scalability mandates in the same sectors (AI, SaaS, fintech, gaming). They deepen the top of the funnel and do not address the domestic Series A/B and later-stage gap.

Working conclusion (to be tested, and modified from the framework you suggested): concentration has a substantial and observable positive effect on the scalability and short-term survival prospects of the small group of recipients; its ecosystem-level effect on the median seed-stage startup is negative in appearance but cannot be causally attributed to concentration itself, because the bottleneck predates it and is better explained by the two-tier structure of capital supply, macro conditions and relocation; the effect on long-term survival cannot be established with existing Turkish data. The essay should also make the point that from 2024 the concentration is increasingly in exits and acquisitions rather than venture rounds, which changes what "VC concentration" means for early-stage firms.

## 5. Problems with the research question

1. "Increasing" is not supported as a monotonic trend. Company-level concentration peaked in 2021 to 2023 and eased in 2025 ("absence of mega-deals"); what increased is the share of value in foreign-led large transactions and, from 2024, in acquisitions. Options: keep the wording but define concentration and the period explicitly in the introduction, or reword to "the concentration of venture capital funding in a small number of Turkish startups, sectors and later-stage rounds".
2. "Survival rates" promises a metric that does not exist for Turkish VC-backed startups. The essay can only use proxies (follow-on funding, later-stage activity, closures, down rounds). Consider "survival prospects" or "survival and scalability" to avoid an unanswerable promise, or keep the wording and state the limitation prominently in the methodology.
3. Two populations are implied. Concentrated capital goes mostly to later-stage or growth companies, while "early-stage startups" are mostly non-recipients. The RQ is really about a spillover from recipients to non-recipients; the essay must frame both populations from the start.
4. Causality. The RQ implies influence; the data are correlational and confounded by macro conditions, the global cycle and relocation. The conclusion must be framed as an extent judgement with explicit alternative explanations.
5. Provider inconsistency. Three providers give three totals for each year. The essay must commit to one series for trends (Startups.watch) and explain why.
6. Subject fit. To be marked as Business Management rather than Economics, the analysis must use business tools where they add value (sources of finance, growth strategies, economies of scale, cash flow, stakeholder conflict) and focus on firm decisions, not only market aggregates.
